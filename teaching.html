01The teacher

Ive, portrait

I have been teaching since I was a second-year student at the conservatory, when a neighbour asked whether I would “just show her daughter the basics”. Ten years later the daughter is studying in The Hague, and I have never stopped teaching.

The most important thing I learned in that time is that nobody quits the piano because it's difficult. People quit because they practise for weeks and can't hear themselves getting better. So my job, more than anything, is to make progress audible — to you, every single week.

That means I don't teach from one method book. A seven-year-old who wants to play the Harry Potter theme and a retired engineer who wants to finally get through the Goldberg aria need completely different routes, even if they arrive at the same place: reading fluently, playing with a free hand, and knowing how to practise on their own.

I am strict about a few things — posture, rhythm, listening — and relaxed about almost everything else. You can bring the pop song you heard on the radio. You can say you hated the piece I gave you. You can tell me you didn't practise at all this week; we'll use the hour to practise together instead.

Most of my students are adults who played as children and stopped, or never started. About a third are children and teenagers, and a handful each year are preparing conservatory auditions. Lessons are in English or Dutch, whichever you prefer.

— Ive

Anatomy of an hour

02Drawn to scale

0′8′20′ — the real work50′
0–8′

Arrive & listen

You play whatever you practised, start to finish, without interruption. I just listen and take notes.

8–20′

Hands

Technique, but always taken from the music in front of us — never scales for their own sake.

20–50′  ·  the real work

Repertoire

The heart of the lesson. We pick the two or three bars that are holding the piece back and solve them together: fingering, sound, pedal, shape. I play a lot here — you should hear what we're aiming for, not just read about it. By the end of this half hour something in the piece should sound different from when you walked in.

50–60′

The week ahead

A short written practice plan: what, in what order, for how long.

A 45-minute lesson keeps the same shape, compressed. Online lessons too — with a second camera on your hands if you have one.

How it develops

03Month, term, year

The first month

Getting comfortable

Sitting, hand shape, reading in both clefs. We find out how you learn best — by ear, by eye, by analysing — and lean into it.

  • One complete short piece
  • A practice routine that fits your week
  • Reading at a slow, steady pulse
The first term

Building a repertoire

Three or four pieces in different styles, chosen together. Technique starts coming from what the pieces need, so it never feels like homework.

  • Hands independent of each other
  • First pedalling and dynamics
  • Sight-reading five minutes a day
The first year

Playing for people

Once a year my students play for each other at an informal house concert — optional, friendly, with cake. For many it's the moment the piano stops being private.

  • A short programme you can perform
  • Learning new pieces on your own
  • Optional ABRSM or conservatory goals
04What I believe
“Talent is mostly attention — and attention can be taught.”

  1. Sound first, notes second.

    A wrong note played beautifully teaches you more than a right note played without listening. We always start from how it should sound.

  2. Slow is not a punishment.

    Practising slowly is the fastest way to learn anything. I'll show you how to make slow practice feel like music, not like waiting.

  3. You should play what you love.

    Bach and Chopin, yes — but also film music, jazz standards, the song from your wedding. Good technique doesn't care about genre.

  4. Every student leaves able to teach themselves.

    The real goal is that one day you won't need me: you'll open a new score and know how to work on it.

  5. Nerves are a skill, not a flaw.

    Performing — even for one friend — is practised like anything else. We prepare for it on purpose, in small steps.
